\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate MLP distinguishers on three ciphers across the following round ranges: \speck (3--8), \simon (4--11), \present (2--7).
Each configuration uses 500K training samples, 3--5 random seeds, and the R2\_xor\_diff representation.
Compute budget: approximately 48 GPU-hours total on a single NVIDIA V100-PCIE-32GB.
Our experimental setting is 2-1-CT-R in the classification of~\citet{bellini2023dbitnet,gerault2024sok}.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare against (i)~our best-bit-bias classical distinguisher, (ii)~Gohr's published ResNet numbers on \speck (92.4\% at 5r, 78.8\% at 6r, 61.6\% at 7r, 51.4\% at 8r)~\citep{gohr2019}, and (iii)~our own ResNet trained under identical conditions.

\Cref{tab:baseline,fig:baseline} reveal a clear \emph{signal depth hierarchy}.
\simon retains distinguishable signal through 9 rounds (52.56\%, CI: [52.30, 52.71]), while \speck saturates at 7 rounds (50.76\%) and \present at 6 rounds (52.55\%, CI: [52.07, 53.04]).

The ordering is consistent with each family's diffusion rate.
\simon's Feistel structure processes only half the state per round, requiring more rounds to destroy signal.
\present's SPN design achieves full diffusion in a single round, eliminating signal most rapidly.
This answers the ``at what depth'' component of RQ1: signal depth is governed by the cipher's diffusion architecture.

Our classical baseline is deliberately weak (single-bit).
Gohr's ResNet substantially outperforms our MLP (61.2\% vs.\ 50.8\% at \speck 7r), but this gap is expected---our contribution is signal characterization, not depth maximization.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{RQ2: Does the Signal Compose Across Rounds?}
\label{sec:exp-rq2}

Classical differential cryptanalysis assumes Markov composition: multi-round differential probabilities factor as products of per-round probabilities, so features valid at $r$ rounds remain valid at $r' < r$.
We test this assumption through transfer experiments and key recovery.

This is the central result of the paper.
\Cref{tab:transfer,fig:transfer} show that Markov composability is family-dependent:

\paragraph{SPN (PRESENT): Markov-composable.}
A 4-round \present distinguisher scores 99.1\% on 2-round data and 96.9\% on 3-round data ($p < 10^{-6}$).
Features learned at higher rounds subsume those at lower rounds---consistent with Markov composition and Gohr et al.'s proof that SPN distinguishers learn only DDT features~\citep{gohr2022assessment}.

\paragraph{ARX (SPECK): Markov-violating.}
A 5-round \speck distinguisher evaluated on 3-round data scores 45.5\%---4.5\pp \emph{below} random chance ($p < 10^{-8}$).
The network has learned features that are not merely irrelevant at fewer rounds, but \emph{negatively correlated} with the lower-round decision boundary.
This is direct evidence of non-Markov, round-specific signal.

\paragraph{Feistel (SIMON): unexpectedly Markov-violating.}
A 6-round \simon distinguisher on 4-round data scores 48.6\% ($p < 10^{-5}$).
Gohr et al.~\citep{gohr2022assessment} proved that Feistel distinguishers should learn only differential features, predicting Markov composition.
The observed anti-transfer contradicts this prediction, suggesting \simon's AND-rotation nonlinearity creates round-dependent differential patterns that disrupt hierarchical composition (\Cref{sec:discussion-rq2}).

\label{sec:exp-key-recovery}

\centering

         Rank is out of 256 candidates per byte.}
\label{tab:key_recovery}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule

Key recovery provides a second, independent test of Markov composition.
Gohr's Bayesian approach~\citep{gohr2019} trains a distinguisher at $r{-}1$ rounds and searches the last-round subkey space, implicitly assuming that the $r$-round signal decomposes into an $(r{-}1)$-round signal plus a key-dependent transformation.

\speck key recovery succeeds: 40\% exact recovery at 5 total rounds (\Cref{tab:key_recovery}).
The signal composes well enough across the last round to support Bayesian scoring---consistent with ARX signal being round-specific but \emph{locally} composable (adjacent rounds share partial features).

\simon key recovery fails completely despite 100\% distinguisher accuracy.
Mean low-byte rank is 122.8/256---indistinguishable from random.
This failure is directly predicted by RQ2: \simon's non-composable features cannot support the implicit Markov assumption in Bayesian subkey search.
The structural cause is that \simon's subkey enters via XOR \emph{after} the nonlinear function $f(x) = (x \lll 1) \mathbin{\&} (x \lll 8) \oplus (x \lll 2)$, entangling subkey errors with the state through a complex nonlinear path.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:exp-summary}

\paragraph{RQ1 (Representation).}
Signal resides in multi-bit XOR-difference channels, persists to family-specific depths (\simon $\gg$ \speck $>$ \present), and is architecture-independent ($\leq$2.6\pp gap across 7 models + ResNet).
It is a property of cipher structure, not model capacity.

\paragraph{RQ2 (Markov).}
Signal composition is family-dependent.
SPN features compose hierarchically (positive transfer, successful decomposition), consistent with the Markov assumption.
ARX and Feistel features are round-specific and anti-compose (below-chance transfer, key recovery failure), violating the Markov assumption.
