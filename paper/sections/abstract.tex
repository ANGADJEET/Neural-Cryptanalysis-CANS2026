\begin{abstract}
Neural differential distinguishers can break more rounds of block ciphers than classical single-bit methods, yet two foundational questions remain open.
\textbf{RQ1 (Representation):} Where does the distinguishing signal reside in observable ciphertext data---in which bits, which representation, and at what depth?
\textbf{RQ2 (Markov):} Does this signal compose across rounds in the memoryless fashion assumed by classical differential cryptanalysis, or do neural networks exploit round-specific, non-composable features?
We answer both questions through a controlled study on three cipher families---\speck (ARX), \simon (Feistel), and \present (SPN)---using seven architectures under identical conditions.
For RQ1, saliency and attention analysis localize signal to XOR-difference bits (bits 14--15, 28--30 in \speck), multi-bit representations outperform word-level inputs by 29\pp, and all seven architectures agree within 2\pp---confirming that signal is a property of cipher structure, not model capacity.
For RQ2, transfer experiments reveal that signal composition is family-dependent: SPN features compose hierarchically (99.1\% transfer, $p < 10^{-11}$), while ARX and Feistel features are round-specific and \emph{anti-compose} (45.5\%, $p < 10^{-8}$).
VAE-based Markov validation confirms this: the memoryless assumption holds through round 4 but breaks at rounds 5--6.
These results fill a gap identified by a recent survey of 66~peer-reviewed papers~\citep{gerault2024sok}: no prior work jointly characterizes signal representation and compositional structure across cipher families.
\end{abstract}
