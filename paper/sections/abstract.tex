\begin{abstract}
Neural differential distinguishers can break more rounds of block ciphers than their classical counterparts, yet every published evaluation targets a single cipher family.
No controlled study has measured whether the features these networks learn \emph{transfer} across structurally different ciphers---leaving open whether neural vulnerability is a property of the cipher or an artifact of the architecture.
We train identical classifiers (MLP, CNN, LSTM, ResNet, and four others) on three cipher families---\speck (ARX), \simon (Feistel), and \present (SPN)---and report three findings.
First, we establish a vulnerability ordering: \simon remains distinguishable through 9 rounds (28\% of full depth), \speck through 7 rounds (32\%), and \present through 6 rounds (19\%), all statistically significant ($p < 0.001$).
Second, we discover that transfer polarity is family-dependent: an ARX distinguisher trained at 5 rounds scores \emph{below chance} (45.5\%, $p < 10^{-8}$) when evaluated at 3 rounds, while an SPN distinguisher trained at 4 rounds scores 99.1\% at 2 rounds.
Third, all seven architectures agree within 2\pp on the same cipher, and ResNet improves over MLP by at most 2.6\pp, confirming that the ordering is intrinsic to cipher structure.
These results fill a gap identified by a recent survey of 66~peer-reviewed papers in neural differential cryptanalysis~\citep{gerault2024sok}: no prior work compares transfer behavior across cipher families under controlled conditions.
\end{abstract}
